1 · Wellbore (TC2)

The pumped CBM well: water up the tubing, gas up the annulus

Annulus water

Water above the pump, aerated by the rising gas (drift flux, Shi et al. 2005), sets the pressure on the coal.

Pump and carryover

A PCP on a variable-speed drive holds 50 ft of water. Fast gas carries water to the surface (Turner, load-corrected).

Inside tNavigator

Each step a Python script writes the well's VFP table from the annulus water. Tank and simulator agree on coal-face pressure to a median 0.14 psi over two years.

Well diagram: RFD tank-model slides · all results on synthetic demo models · equations in the backup

The model is called the annulus tank model: 'tank' is the water inventory in the annulus, not a surface vessel. Per well: casing and tubing sizes, PCP displacement and slip, VSD limits 50 to 400 rpm, drift-flux gas holdup, Turner critical velocity with the Zhou and Yuan liquid-load correction, finite spill and a flowline delay for carried water. Coupling: APPLYSCRIPT writes a VFPPROD table per well (coal-face pressure against casing-head pressure and water rate, at the end-of-step water volume), so reservoir inflow and annulus filling are solved together; a network action passes pump and carried water to Network Designer. The native multisegment drift-flux option was tested first: in 26.2 its holdup did not change the wellbore pressure drop, so the tank model was built. Median mismatch 0.14 psi: a two-year run (730 days), 21 random pump stops.

Synthetic well W3, day 95 of a two-year run with random pump stops · tank model · tNavigator

W3 on day 95: the pump stops for 15 h. The clear-water height above the intake rises from 50 ft to about 970 ft and the coal-face pressure from 52 to 403 psia (the simulator's WBHP follows the tank model). The lower seam stops producing after about 2.5 h, when the wellbore pressure there exceeds the seam pressure; the upper seam keeps delivering a declining rate, so the gas falls from 1,200 to under 50 Mscf/d. Wellbore crossflow is switched off in this run: with a water-filled wellbore the lower seam cannot take gas back. On restart the VSD runs 240 rpm, the level is back at 50 ft within about 2 hours, the gas flushes to about 1,600 Mscf/d and settles near 1,250.

2 · Complex wells through simple IPRs

The network wells carry the integrated model's rates and decline

Each IPR, q = C (P² − p²), is set from the integrated model: within 1-3% at peak for six of seven well types.

Under control both hold the 30,000 target. With the decline added as P(t) the plateau ends near day 330, as in the integrated model.

IPR in a Python object per well · decline P(t) from the integrated model's free run · 70-well network, target 30,000 Mscf/d

Left: the network demo's base IPRs against the integrated model (no control) at about 35 psia wellhead on day 60: W1 1,040 vs 1,019, W3 1,274 vs 1,250, W5 1,715 vs 1,722, and so on. W2 is the exception (301 vs 159): in the integrated model W2 is weak through a low C, and C sits inside each Python object, which the workflow API cannot edit, so W2 keeps its level and gets only the decline shape. Right: the decline is added as the source pressure P(t) of every well, P(t)^2 = 35^2 + (P0^2 - 35^2) r(t), with r(t) from the integrated model's free run; the controller uses the same P(t). A daily calendar is needed (the delivered calendar is monthly between events). 70 wells: target held within 1% to about day 330, 26,781 Mscf/d on day 365. 700 wells: same shape, 266,974 on day 365.

2 · Water

Water separated at every wellhead, gathered in a parallel network

Each well's water follows its own water-to-gas ratio from the integrated model, high during dewatering and falling with time.

A separator on every wellhead sends the water through pad and collector lines, laid along the gas routes, to a treatment plant.

The scheme is laid out by collector line and zone, so the network reads on screen.

70-well network after the water extension · 70 wellhead separators · 1 treatment plant (700 wells: 700 separators, 2 plants)

Water: each source gets a water-gas ratio WGR(t) per well type (stb/Mscf), chosen so that at 35 psia wellhead the water equals the integrated model's (no control) coal-water rate: about 57 bbl/Mscf on day 1, 0.8 on day 35, 0.2 by day 180. With a constant WGR per date, choking a well also cuts its water, which is the lever the optimiser uses. Network: a two-phase separator is inserted between each IPR object and its choke; its water outlet links to a pad water node; pad, collector and zone water lines mirror the gas gathering lines (same lengths), 6 to 10 inch, flat, Mukherjee-Brill; zone mains end at a plant inlet node and the plant sink at 20 psia. The 700-well network has two plants (zones 1-5 north, 6-10 south) and ten zone groups. Layout: collector lines as a fish-bone, pads above and below each line, the water line drawn parallel to the gas line.

2 · Water-limited optimisation

The plant limit holds, and the gas comes from the driest wells first

20,000 bbl/d plant limit held from day 1 to day 45; unlimited peak 77,600

Day 45: gas on the 30,000 target, water just under the limit

Cost: 1,047 vs 1,794 MMscf gas in the first 60 days

Same at 700 wells with two plants (100,000 and 70,000 bbl/d): both limits held within 0.3%, target reached on day 49.

70-well network with decline and water · one Network Designer Python action · 366 daily steps

Controller extension: each well's water is WGR x gas; each plant has a capacity. Allocation per date: q_i = min(qmax_i, L_gas, L_water / WGR_i). L_gas is the balancing level that meets the gas target; when a plant would exceed its capacity, a water level L_water caps the wettest wells first (their water is levelled), the drier wells keep L_gas, and L_gas rises to recover the target where the other wells and plants allow. While the plant binds the controller takes a full step (up to 200 psi a day on a choke) instead of the smooth 40% step. Result (70 wells, plant 20,000 bbl/d): water never more than 2% over the limit; without the limit water peaks at 77,600 bbl/d on day 20; with the limit the gas ramps slower and reaches the 30,000 Mscf/d target on day 45; after day 60 both runs are the same. Limitation: the decline is scheduled in time, so holding water back does not delay dewatering in this model; in a real coal it would. 700 wells, two plants (WTP_NORTH 100,000 and WTP_SOUTH 70,000 bbl/d): both limits held within 0.3%; without limits the plants would peak at about 391,000 and 385,000 bbl/d around day 20; the 300,000 Mscf/d target is reached on day 49; gas in the first 60 days 9,277 vs 17,439 MMscf; the same as the unlimited run after day 60.

4 · Proxy research · how it works

The proxy predicts a few coefficients per well, not a time series

1

Wells as a graph

Node = a well: BHP, permeability, depth. Edge = a neighbour: mean permeability, porosity, distance. Static inputs from the tNavigator model, plus each well's production history.

2

Graph neural network

Graph convolutions pass information between neighbouring wells, so interference is learned from the layout.

3

8 + 12 coefficients per well

Output: weights of 8 gas and 12 water basis functions (right). Their sum is a smooth 240-month gas and water profile.

Trained on tNavigator runs: 12 wells, 240 months, 2 wells held out for validation. Training about 2 minutes, a forecast in 2 seconds.

UNSW MUTRIS group, tNav project update, 16 September 2026 · basis-function figure from their slides

From the UNSW update of 16 September 2026, slides 5-9. Approach: each well is a node with features (BHP, permeability, depth); edges connect adjacent wells and carry features (mean permeability, porosity, distance); the static inputs are the topology of the tNavigator test model, the dynamic inputs its production history. The network outputs coefficients Kg (8 gas) and Kw (12 water) of fixed basis functions; together with the basis functions they give smooth production profiles, which keeps the output small and the forecast stable. Test case: a tNavigator reservoir model with 12 wells simulated for 240 months. Preliminary: 2 wells reserved for validation, no hyperparameter optimisation, training about 2 minutes, inference 2 seconds. Next steps they list: more training data, a wider Surat-type parameter space, different well arrangements, Queensland Government water production data.

4 · UNSW proposal

A control-aware proxy for the reservoir, with tNavigator still solving the network

Why

At low gathering pressure a choke change moves the whole network at once and the coal later. A coupled run is too slow to try many settings.

What

A graph network replaces the reservoir grid: wells are nodes, edges join wells whose drainage areas touch. The surface network stays in tNavigator.

Open questions

Does it handle events it has not seen, like a pump stop? Does it run on a field where wells were added or shut in, without retraining?

It receives the planned controls with the history and forecasts gas and water per well for the coming months. It is trained on runs with varied control schedules, the kind our integrated models produce.

UNSW-RFD problem statement: a control-aware AI-based subsurface proxy for coupled reservoir and surface-network simulation in CBM

From the UNSW-RFD problem statement. Architecture: a spatiotemporal graph neural network; each well a node; subsurface edges between wells whose Voronoi drainage areas share a boundary; no surface edges, because tNavigator resolves the pipe network implicitly and the proxy only learns the subsurface response; a recurrent encoder per node for production histories. Control-aware: it receives the control settings, including those planned for the forecast period; unplanned events such as a pump shutdown are treated as a control change that has just happened. Static properties, seismic, geology and drainage volumes can be fed into nodes and edges, which leaves room for stress- and desorption-driven permeability change. This matches the offer's TC6 architecture 2 (subsurface proxy feeding a physical network solve).
